Two things I learned, as toys

Both exhibits run in your browser on illustrations I drew. Nothing here is patient data.

Grade a retina

Diabetic retinopathy is graded from 0 to 4. Pick a grade to see what changes in the eye, then look at how many examples of each grade the dataset has.

No DR

Images per grade in APTOS 2019 (3,662 labelled images). Tap a bar to select it. The retina above is an illustration, not a real photograph.

Rebuild a slice

My reconstruction pipeline starts by keeping only the voxels in the right Hounsfield range, then cleaning the result. Move the threshold and watch the surface appear, break or vanish.

300 HU

The slice is a synthetic head phantom with assumed Hounsfield values and added noise. The scale is named after Sir Godfrey Hounsfield, who built the first CT scanner and shared the 1979 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine.

✦｡ﾟ☾ ｡⋆ vision

Where this is going

I want to build medical imaging tools that doctors can trust. That means models that are tested honestly, images that are reconstructed with care, and systems designed around the rules of medical devices from day one. I am approaching it from both ends: hospital imaging rooms on one side, PyTorch notebooks on the other.

Models that admit their limits

My retinal model scored well overall and poorly on the rarest class. I want to work on evaluation that exposes weak spots early: honest splits, independent test sets and reliability checks.

From slices to shapes

CT and MRI arrive as stacks of flat images. I am drawn to turning them into 3D models that clinicians and engineers can inspect, measure and print.

Imaging that fits the clinic

Positioning a child, an elderly patient or someone who cannot move showed me that every algorithm depends on the image someone managed to acquire. I want my work to respect that.
